Boosting Algorithms: Step-by-Step Working

AdaBoost  •  Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM)  •  Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)

Boosting is a sequential ensemble learning technique in which multiple weak learners (typically shallow decision trees) are combined to form one strong learner. Unlike bagging, where models are trained independently in parallel, boosting trains models one after another — every new learner focuses on correcting the mistakes made by the previous ones. The final prediction is a weighted combination of all the weak learners.

Overall Role of Boosting: To reduce bias (and, to a lesser extent, variance) by iteratively emphasizing hard-to-predict examples, converting a collection of "weak" models (barely better than random guessing) into one highly accurate "strong" model.

1. AdaBoost (Adaptive Boosting)

Role: AdaBoost builds a strong classifier by sequentially training weak learners (usually decision stumps), where each new learner pays more attention to the samples that were misclassified by earlier learners — achieved by adaptively re-weighting the training data. Objective: Minimize the exponential loss over the training set.
Objective Function (Exponential Loss)
$$ L = \sum_{i=1}^{N} e^{-y_i F(x_i)} \qquad \text{where } F(x)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_t h_t(x) $$
Term-by-Term Explanation
\(L\)
— the total exponential loss over the whole training set; this is what AdaBoost is trying to minimize.
\(N\)
— total number of training samples.
\(y_i\)
— true class label of sample \(i\), encoded as \(+1\) or \(-1\) (not 0/1).
\(F(x_i)\)
— the combined (ensemble) model's output for sample \(i\), i.e. the weighted sum of all weak learners so far.
\(T\)
— total number of boosting rounds (i.e. number of weak learners trained).
\(\alpha_t\)
— the "vote weight" given to weak learner \(t\); learners with lower error get a larger \(\alpha_t\).
\(h_t(x)\)
— the prediction of the \(t\)-th weak learner (e.g. a decision stump) on input \(x\), output as \(+1\) or \(-1\).

Step-by-Step Working

1
Initialize sample weights equally for all N training points.
Step 1
$$ w_i^{(1)} = \dfrac{1}{N}, \quad i=1,2,\dots,N $$
Term-by-Term Explanation
\(w_i^{(1)}\)
— the weight (importance) assigned to sample \(i\) at round 1 (the very first round).
\(N\)
— total number of training samples, so every sample starts with equal importance \(1/N\).
2
Train a weak learner \(h_t(x)\) (e.g. a decision stump) on the weighted training data.
3
Compute the weighted error rate of that weak learner.
Step 3
$$ \varepsilon_t = \dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i^{(t)}\, \mathbb{1}(y_i \neq h_t(x_i))}{\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i^{(t)}} $$
Term-by-Term Explanation
\(\varepsilon_t\)
— the weighted error rate of weak learner \(t\); how "wrong" it is, after accounting for which samples currently matter most.
\(w_i^{(t)}\)
— the current weight (importance) of sample \(i\) at round \(t\).
\(\mathbb{1}(y_i \neq h_t(x_i))\)
— an indicator function that equals 1 if the learner misclassifies sample \(i\), and 0 if it classifies correctly.
Denominator
— the sum of all current weights, used to normalize the error into a proper rate between 0 and 1.
4
Compute learner's voting weight (alpha) — a lower error gives a higher alpha, meaning more trustworthy learners get a louder "vote" in the final decision.
Step 4
$$ \alpha_t = \dfrac{1}{2}\ln\!\left(\dfrac{1-\varepsilon_t}{\varepsilon_t}\right) $$
Term-by-Term Explanation
\(\alpha_t\)
— the vote weight (confidence) given to weak learner \(t\) in the final ensemble.
\(\varepsilon_t\)
— the weighted error rate of learner \(t\) from Step 3, used to decide how much to trust it.
\(\ln(\cdot)\)
— natural logarithm; as \(\varepsilon_t \to 0\), \(\alpha_t \to \infty\) (very trustworthy); as \(\varepsilon_t \to 0.5\), \(\alpha_t \to 0\) (no better than random guessing).
5
Update sample weights — increase weight of misclassified points, decrease it for correctly classified ones, then normalize so weights sum to 1.
Step 5
$$ w_i^{(t+1)} = \dfrac{w_i^{(t)}\, e^{-\alpha_t y_i h_t(x_i)}}{Z_t} $$
Term-by-Term Explanation
\(w_i^{(t+1)}\)
— the updated weight of sample \(i\) going into the next round \(t+1\).
\(y_i h_t(x_i)\)
— equals \(+1\) if the learner classified sample \(i\) correctly, and \(-1\) if it misclassified it (since both are \(\pm1\)).
\(e^{-\alpha_t y_i h_t(x_i)}\)
— greater than 1 for misclassified samples (weight increases) and less than 1 for correctly classified samples (weight decreases).
\(Z_t\)
— a normalization constant chosen so that all updated weights \(w_i^{(t+1)}\) sum to 1.
6
Repeat steps 2–5 for \(t = 1, 2, \dots, T\) rounds (T weak learners).
7
Combine all weak learners into the final strong classifier using a weighted majority vote.
Step 7 — Final Model
$$ H(x) = \text{sign}\!\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T} \alpha_t\, h_t(x)\right) $$
Term-by-Term Explanation
\(H(x)\)
— the final strong classifier's prediction for input \(x\).
\(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_t h_t(x)\)
— a weighted vote across all \(T\) weak learners, each learner's opinion \(h_t(x)\) scaled by how trustworthy it is (\(\alpha_t\)).
\(\text{sign}(\cdot)\)
— converts the weighted sum into a final class label: \(+1\) if positive, \(-1\) if negative.

1.1 AdaBoost Applied to Dataset A (Classification)

Using Dataset A (Pass/Fail), Round 1 of AdaBoost proceeds as follows:

1
All 10 students start with equal weight \(w_i = 1/10 = 0.1\).
2
A decision stump is trained, e.g. the rule "Hours_Studied ≥ 4.5 → Pass". Suppose it misclassifies student #6 (studied 3 hrs but the stump predicts Fail correctly — assume instead it wrongly predicts on student #10).
3
Weighted error with 1 misclassified point out of 10 equal weights:
Numeric Example
$$ \varepsilon_1 = \dfrac{0.1}{1.0} = 0.1 $$
4
Voting weight for this stump:
Numeric Example
$$ \alpha_1 = \dfrac{1}{2}\ln\!\left(\dfrac{1-0.1}{0.1}\right) = \dfrac{1}{2}\ln(9) \approx 1.10 $$
5
Weight of the misclassified student is boosted (multiplied by \(e^{\alpha_1}\)), all others shrink (multiplied by \(e^{-\alpha_1}\)), then all weights are renormalized to sum to 1 before Round 2 begins.

This process repeats for T rounds, each time producing a new stump that focuses harder on previously-hard cases; the final label is the weighted-majority sign of all stumps. Note: AdaBoost can also be used for regression via the AdaBoost.R2 variant, which reweights based on the magnitude of prediction error rather than misclassification.

2. Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM)

Role: GBM builds an ensemble by having every new tree fit the residual errors (more precisely, the negative gradient of a chosen loss function) of the combined model built so far. Unlike AdaBoost's sample-reweighting, GBM directly performs gradient descent in function space. Objective: Minimize any differentiable loss function \(L(y, F(x))\), e.g. squared error for regression, log-loss for classification.
General Objective
$$ F^{*} = \arg\min_{F} \; \sum_{i=1}^{N} L\big(y_i, F(x_i)\big) $$
Term-by-Term Explanation
\(F^{*}\)
— the best possible model function, the one that minimizes total loss over the training data.
\(\arg\min_F\)
— "the value of \(F\) that gives the minimum"; we are searching over possible models, not just numbers.
\(L(y_i,F(x_i))\)
— the loss function measuring how far prediction \(F(x_i)\) is from the true value \(y_i\) for sample \(i\).
\(N\)
— total number of training samples, summed over to get total loss.
Squared-Error Loss (used for our regression example)
$$ L(y_i, F(x_i)) = \tfrac{1}{2}\big(y_i - F(x_i)\big)^2 $$
Term-by-Term Explanation
\(y_i\)
— the true target value for sample \(i\) (e.g. actual house price).
\(F(x_i)\)
— the model's current predicted value for sample \(i\).
\((y_i-F(x_i))^2\)
— squares the error so that positive and negative errors don't cancel and larger errors are penalized more.
\(\tfrac{1}{2}\)
— a convenience constant included purely so the derivative of the loss simplifies cleanly to \((y_i-F(x_i))\).

2.1 Gradient Boosting — Step-by-Step Working

1
Initialize the model with a constant value that minimizes the loss over all training targets (for squared error, this is simply the mean of y).
Step 1
$$ F_0(x) = \arg\min_{\gamma}\sum_{i=1}^{N} L(y_i,\gamma) $$
Term-by-Term Explanation
\(F_0(x)\)
— the initial model, a constant prediction (same value regardless of \(x\)).
\(\gamma\)
— a single constant value being optimized; for squared-error loss this works out to the mean of all \(y_i\).
\(\arg\min_\gamma\)
— find the constant \(\gamma\) that minimizes total loss across all N samples before any trees are added.
2
Compute pseudo-residuals — the negative gradient of the loss with respect to the current prediction. For squared error this reduces to the simple residual \(y_i - F(x_i)\).
Step 2
$$ r_{im} = -\left[\dfrac{\partial L(y_i, F(x_i))}{\partial F(x_i)}\right]_{F=F_{m-1}} $$
Term-by-Term Explanation
\(r_{im}\)
— the pseudo-residual for sample \(i\) at boosting round \(m\); the "error direction" the next tree should learn to fix.
\(\partial L/\partial F(x_i)\)
— the gradient (rate of change) of the loss with respect to the prediction; tells us how the loss would change if the prediction changed slightly.
Negative sign
— we move opposite to the gradient (gradient descent), i.e. in the direction that most reduces the loss.
\(F=F_{m-1}\)
— the gradient is evaluated using the model built so far (after \(m-1\) rounds), before the new tree is added.
3
Fit a regression tree \(h_m(x)\) to the pseudo-residuals \(r_{im}\) (the tree learns to predict the current error, not the original target).
4
Compute the optimal step size (multiplier) for each leaf region \(R_{jm}\) via line search.
Step 4
$$ \gamma_{jm} = \arg\min_{\gamma} \sum_{x_i \in R_{jm}} L\big(y_i,\, F_{m-1}(x_i)+\gamma\big) $$
Term-by-Term Explanation
\(\gamma_{jm}\)
— the best constant output value for leaf region \(j\) in tree \(m\) (how much this leaf should push the prediction up or down).
\(R_{jm}\)
— the \(j\)-th leaf region (a group of samples) of tree \(m\), determined by the tree's split rules.
\(x_i \in R_{jm}\)
— we sum only over samples that fall into this particular leaf.
\(F_{m-1}(x_i)+\gamma\)
— the candidate new prediction if we add constant \(\gamma\) to the existing model's output.
5
Update the model by adding the new tree, scaled by a learning rate \(\nu\) (shrinkage) that controls how much each tree contributes — smaller \(\nu\) reduces overfitting but needs more trees.
Step 5
$$ F_m(x) = F_{m-1}(x) + \nu \sum_{j} \gamma_{jm}\, \mathbb{1}(x \in R_{jm}) $$
Term-by-Term Explanation
\(F_m(x)\)
— the updated ensemble model after adding tree \(m\).
\(F_{m-1}(x)\)
— the model built so far, before this round's tree is added.
\(\nu\)
— the learning rate (shrinkage), a small value like 0.1 that scales down each tree's contribution to prevent overfitting.
\(\gamma_{jm}\mathbb{1}(x\in R_{jm})\)
— for input \(x\), find which leaf region \(R_{jm}\) it falls into and add that leaf's value \(\gamma_{jm}\).
6
Repeat steps 2–5 for \(m=1,\dots,M\) boosting rounds to obtain the final additive model \(F_M(x)\).

3. Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)

Role: XGBoost is an optimized, regularized, and highly scalable implementation of gradient boosting. It improves on plain GBM by using a second-order Taylor expansion of the loss (faster, more accurate convergence), adding an explicit regularization term to control tree complexity, and using an efficient exact/approximate greedy algorithm to find the best splits. Objective: Minimize loss + model complexity penalty simultaneously, which improves generalization and prevents overfitting.
Regularized Objective
$$ \text{Obj} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} l(y_i, \hat{y}_i) \;+\; \sum_{k=1}^{K} \Omega(f_k) $$ $$ \Omega(f) = \gamma T + \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda \sum_{j=1}^{T} w_j^2 $$
Term-by-Term Explanation
\(\text{Obj}\)
— total objective to minimize: prediction error plus a penalty for model complexity.
\(l(y_i,\hat y_i)\)
— the training loss between true value \(y_i\) and prediction \(\hat y_i\) (e.g. squared error or log-loss).
\(K\)
— the total number of trees currently in the ensemble.
\(\Omega(f_k)\)
— the regularization penalty for tree \(f_k\), discouraging overly complex trees.
\(\gamma\)
— penalty per leaf; a higher \(\gamma\) means the algorithm needs more Gain to justify creating an extra leaf.
\(T\)
— number of leaves in the tree; more leaves means more complexity and a larger \(\gamma T\) penalty.
\(\lambda\)
— L2 regularization strength on leaf weights; higher \(\lambda\) shrinks leaf weights \(w_j\) towards zero, making the model more conservative.
\(w_j\)
— the output (weight) of leaf \(j\), i.e. the value predicted for any sample that lands in that leaf.

Here \(T\) is the number of leaves in a tree, \(w_j\) are leaf weights, and \(\gamma, \lambda\) are regularization hyperparameters penalizing tree complexity.

Step-by-Step Working

1
Initialize the model with a base prediction (e.g. mean of y, same as GBM).
2
Compute first and second order gradients of the loss for every training point with respect to the current prediction.
Step 2
$$ g_i = \dfrac{\partial l(y_i,\hat{y}_i^{(m-1)})}{\partial \hat{y}_i^{(m-1)}}, \qquad h_i = \dfrac{\partial^2 l(y_i,\hat{y}_i^{(m-1)})}{\partial (\hat{y}_i^{(m-1)})^2} $$
Term-by-Term Explanation
\(g_i\)
— first-order gradient of the loss for sample \(i\); same role as the residual direction in plain GBM.
\(h_i\)
— second-order gradient (the "hessian") for sample \(i\); captures the curvature of the loss, which XGBoost uses that GBM ignores.
\(\hat y_i^{(m-1)}\)
— the model's prediction for sample \(i\) after \(m-1\) rounds, i.e. before the current tree is added.
\(\partial l/\partial \hat y_i\)
— how much the loss changes as the prediction changes (1st derivative).
\(\partial^2 l/\partial \hat y_i^2\)
— how the rate of change itself changes (2nd derivative); used to better calibrate the step size for each leaf.
3
Approximate the objective using a second-order Taylor expansion — this is the key innovation that gives XGBoost both speed and accuracy over first-order GBM.
Step 3
$$ \text{Obj}^{(m)} \approx \sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[g_i f_m(x_i) + \tfrac{1}{2}h_i f_m(x_i)^2\right] + \Omega(f_m) $$
Term-by-Term Explanation
\(\text{Obj}^{(m)}\)
— the approximate objective for round \(m\), rewritten in terms of gradients so it becomes easy to optimize.
\(f_m(x_i)\)
— the output of the new tree being built this round, for sample \(i\) (this is what we are solving for).
\(g_i f_m(x_i)\)
— the linear (1st-order) term: how much the loss changes proportional to the new tree's output.
\(\tfrac12 h_i f_m(x_i)^2\)
— the quadratic (2nd-order) term capturing curvature, giving a more accurate approximation than gradient-only methods like GBM.
\(\Omega(f_m)\)
— the regularization penalty for the new tree, keeping it simple and preventing overfitting.
4
Grow the tree greedily, evaluating every candidate split using the closed-form Gain formula (derived by optimizing the Taylor-expanded objective per leaf); a split is kept only if Gain > 0.
Step 4 — Split Gain
$$ \text{Gain} = \tfrac{1}{2}\left[\dfrac{G_L^2}{H_L+\lambda} + \dfrac{G_R^2}{H_R+\lambda} - \dfrac{(G_L+G_R)^2}{H_L+H_R+\lambda}\right] - \gamma $$
Term-by-Term Explanation
\(\text{Gain}\)
— how much the objective improves by making this particular split; higher Gain means a better split.
\(G_L, G_R\)
— sum of gradients \(g_i\) for samples that would fall into the left / right child node after the split.
\(H_L, H_R\)
— sum of hessians \(h_i\) for samples in the left / right child node.
\(\frac{G_L^2}{H_L+\lambda}\), \(\frac{G_R^2}{H_R+\lambda}\)
— the "score" (how well-fit the loss is) of the left and right leaves separately, after the split.
\(\frac{(G_L+G_R)^2}{H_L+H_R+\lambda}\)
— the score of the single combined node before splitting; subtracting this checks whether splitting actually helps.
\(\lambda\)
— regularization term that smooths scores and discourages splits supported by very few, low-confidence samples.
\(\gamma\)
— fixed cost charged for adding this split (creating one more leaf); the split is only kept if Gain exceeds this cost.
5
Compute the optimal weight for each leaf once the tree structure is fixed.
Step 5 — Leaf Weight
$$ w_j^{*} = -\dfrac{G_j}{H_j+\lambda} $$
Term-by-Term Explanation
\(w_j^{*}\)
— the optimal output value assigned to leaf \(j\) once the tree's structure (splits) is finalized.
\(G_j\)
— sum of gradients \(g_i\) over all samples that land in leaf \(j\).
\(H_j\)
— sum of hessians \(h_i\) over all samples that land in leaf \(j\).
\(\lambda\)
— regularization constant that shrinks the leaf weight towards zero, preventing overconfident predictions from leaves with few samples.
Negative sign
— comes directly from minimizing the quadratic approximation of the loss for that leaf.
6
Update predictions with a learning rate \(\eta\), then repeat steps 2–5 for M rounds; extras such as column subsampling, row subsampling, and shrinkage further reduce overfitting.
Step 6
$$ \hat{y}_i^{(m)} = \hat{y}_i^{(m-1)} + \eta \, f_m(x_i) $$
Term-by-Term Explanation
\(\hat y_i^{(m)}\)
— the updated prediction for sample \(i\) after adding the \(m\)-th tree.
\(\hat y_i^{(m-1)}\)
— the prediction before this round's tree was added.
\(\eta\)
— the learning rate; scales down each new tree's contribution (same role as \(\nu\) in plain GBM) to improve generalization.
\(f_m(x_i)\)
— the output of the newly built tree \(m\) for sample \(i\), i.e. the leaf weight \(w_j^{*}\) of the leaf that \(x_i\) falls into.
